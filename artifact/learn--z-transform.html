Part I — the z-transform

The z-transform is to the DTFT what the Laplace transform is to the CTFT. It extends analysis to more signals, handles unstable systems, and reveals causality and stability through poles, zeros and the ROC. The difference is geometric: the Fourier transform lives on the jωj\omegajω axis of the s-plane; the DTFT lives on the unit circle of the z-plane.

\end{aligned}$$ **Why $z$ instead of $e^{s}$?** In the DTFT we set $T=1$ and work with $e^{j\omega n}$; we could generalise the same way and write $e^{sn}$. But the map $s\mapsto e^{sT}$ is **many-to-one**: shifting $\omega$ by $2\pi/T$ gives the same $z$. In continuous time $e^{j(\omega+2\pi/T)t}\neq e^{j\omega t}$ for general $t$, so distinct $s$ still give distinct signals. In discrete time $n$ is an integer, so $e^{j(\omega+2\pi/T)Tn}=e^{j\omega Tn}$: different $s$ values produce the **same** discrete signal. Using $z$ as the parameter collapses this redundancy, and makes the algebra and geometry much more intuitive. ### How the mapping changes the geometry | s-plane | | z-plane | |---|---|---| | vertical line $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}=\sigma_0$ | → | circle $\lvert z\rvert=e^{\sigma_0T}$ | | $j\omega$ axis ($\sigma=0$) | → | unit circle $\lvert z\rvert=1$ | | ROC strip | → | ROC ring (annulus) | ```fig z-mapping ``` The unit circle plays the role the $j\omega$ axis plays in the s-plane. ROC questions become questions about **radii**, not real parts. ## The system function $H(z)$

H(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}h[k]z^{-k},\qquad x[n]=z^n\Rightarrow y[n]=H(z)z^n .

- Convolution in time becomes multiplication in $z$: $Y(z)=H(z)X(z)$ (the parallel of $Y(s)=H(s)X(s)$). - Poles of $H(z)$ are the natural modes of the system; zeros are the frequencies it blocks. ## Connection to the DTFT Write $z=re^{j\omega}$: $$\begin{aligned}&X(re^{j\omega})\\[6pt]&= \sum_nx[n](re^{j\omega})^{-n}\\[6pt]&= \sum_n\big(x[n]r^{-n}\big)e^{-j\omega n}\\[6pt]&= \mathcal F\{x[n]r^{-n}\}.\end{aligned}$$ Setting $r=1$ restricts the z-transform to the unit circle and recovers the DTFT. **The DTFT exists iff the ROC includes $|z|=1$.** # Part II — examples and the ROC ## Example 10.1 — a right-sided exponential $x[n]=a^nu[n]$: $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^nz^{-n}\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(az^{-1})^n\\[6pt]&= \frac1{1-az^{-1}}\\[6pt]&= \frac z{z-a},\qquad|az^{-1}|<1\iff|z|>|a| . \end{aligned}$$ - The discrete analogue of $e^{-at}u(t)$. - Pole at $z=a$, zero at $z=0$. - The ROC is the **exterior of a circle**; if $|a|<1$ it includes the unit circle and the DTFT exists. ```fig z-ex101 ``` ## Example 10.2 — a left-sided exponential $x[n]=-a^nu[-n-1]$: $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)\\[6pt]&= -\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}a^nz^{-n}\\[6pt]&= -\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(a^{-1}z)^m\\[6pt]&= 1-\frac1{1-a^{-1}z}\\[6pt]&= \frac1{1-az^{-1}}\\[6pt]&= \frac z{z-a},\qquad|z|<|a| . \end{aligned}$$ The same algebraic expression, but the ROC is the **interior** of the circle. Exactly the message from Laplace: the algebraic form alone is not enough. ```fig z-ex102 ``` ## Why the ROC matters A z-transform is specified by **algebraic expression + ROC**. For rational transforms the zeros are the roots of the numerator and the poles the roots of the denominator; the ROC tells us whether the sequence is right-sided, left-sided or two-sided, whether the DTFT exists, and (later) whether a system is causal or stable. ```fig z-shapes ``` ## Example 10.3 — sum of two exponentials $x[n]=7\left(\frac13\right)^nu[n]-6\left(\frac12\right)^nu[n]$. By linearity and Example 10.1: $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)\\[6pt]&= \frac7{1-\frac13z^{-1}}-\frac6{1-\frac12z^{-1}}\\[6pt]&= \frac{1-\frac32z^{-1}}{\left(1-\frac13z^{-1}\right)\left(1-\frac12z^{-1}\right)}\\[6pt]&= \frac{z\left(z-\frac32\right)}{\left(z-\frac13\right)\left(z-\frac12\right)}.\end{aligned}$$ Both components are right-sided, so the ROC is $|z|>\frac12$: outside the outermost pole. Poles at $\frac13,\frac12$; zeros at $0$ and $\frac32$ (from the numerator $7\left(1-\frac12z^{-1}\right)-6\left(1-\frac13z^{-1}\right)=1-\frac32z^{-1}$). ```fig z-ex103 ``` ## Example 10.4 — a damped sinusoid $x[n]=\left(\frac13\right)^n\sin\left(\frac\pi4n\right)u[n]$. With Euler, $\sin\frac\pi4n=\frac{e^{j\pi n/4}-e^{-j\pi n/4}}{2j}$: $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)\\[6pt]&= \frac1{2j}\frac1{1-\frac13e^{j\pi/4}z^{-1}}-\frac1{2j}\frac1{1-\frac13e^{-j\pi/4}z^{-1}}\\[6pt]&= \frac{\frac{\sqrt2}{6}z^{-1}}{1-\frac{\sqrt2}3z^{-1}+\frac19z^{-2}}\\[6pt]&= \frac{\frac{\sqrt2}6z}{\left(z-\frac13e^{j\pi/4}\right)\left(z-\frac13e^{-j\pi/4}\right)}.\end{aligned}$$ Both components are right-sided: ROC $|z|>\frac13$. ```fig z-ex104 ``` ## ROC properties **Property 1.** The ROC is a **ring centred at the origin**. Absolute convergence, $\sum|x[n]|r^{-n}<\infty$, depends on the radius $r=|z|$, not on the angle. **Property 2.** The ROC **cannot contain any poles**. Zeros may lie in the ROC; poles only on its boundary or outside it. **Property 3 — finite-duration sequences.** The ROC is the entire z-plane, **except possibly $z=0$ and/or $z=\infty$**. Only finitely many terms appear, $X(z)=\sum_{n=N_1}^{N_2}x[n]z^{-n}$, so every finite non-zero $z$ is safe. But: - a sample at $n>0$ creates a negative power of $z$, which blows up as $|z|\to0$: it can exclude $z=0$; - a sample at $n<0$ creates a positive power of $z$, which blows up as $|z|\to\infty$: it can exclude $z=\infty$. So if $N_1\ge0$ the ROC includes $\infty$; if $N_2\le0$ it includes 0. **Example 10.5.** $\delta[n]\leftrightarrow1$ (all $z$, including 0 and $\infty$); $\delta[n-1]\leftrightarrow z^{-1}$ (all $z$ except 0); $\delta[n+1]\leftrightarrow z$ (all finite $z$, but not $\infty$). **Property 4 — right-sided.** If $x[n]=0$ for $n<N_1$ and the circle $|z|=r_0$ is in the ROC, then every finite $z$ with $|z|>r_0$ is too. *Why:* for $r_1>r_0$, $|x[n]|r_1^{-n}=|x[n]|r_0^{-n}\left(\frac{r_0}{r_1}\right)^n$, and the extra factor decays as $n\to\infty$. (If $N_1<0$, $\infty$ may be excluded; for causal sequences $\infty$ is in the ROC.) **Property 5 — left-sided.** If $x[n]=0$ for $n>N_2$ and $|z|=r_0$ is in the ROC, then every $z$ with $0<|z|<r_0$ is too. *Why:* now $r_0/r_1>1$ but $n\to-\infty$, so the factor still decays. (If $N_2>0$, 0 may be excluded; for anticausal sequences 0 is in the ROC.) **Property 6 — two-sided.** If $x[n]$ is two-sided and has a z-transform, the ROC is an **annulus**. Split $x=x\,u[n-N]+x\,(1-u[n-N])$: the right-sided part gives an exterior, the left-sided part an interior. The z-transform exists only if they overlap, and the overlap is a ring $r_1<|z|<r_2$. ## Example 10.6 — a finite-length exponential $x[n]=a^n$ for $0\le n\le N-1$ ($a>0$): $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a^nz^{-n}\\[6pt]&= \frac{1-(az^{-1})^N}{1-az^{-1}}\\[6pt]&= \frac1{z^{N-1}}\cdot\frac{z^N-a^N}{z-a}.\end{aligned}$$ The sequence is finite, so the ROC is all $z$ except possibly 0. The roots of $z^N=a^N$ are $z_k=ae^{j2\pi k/N}$; the one at $k=0$ cancels the factor $z-a$. What remains: - zeros at $z_k=ae^{j2\pi k/N}$, $k=1,\dots,N-1$, **evenly spaced on a circle of radius $a$**; - $N-1$ poles at the origin, so the ROC is $|z|>0$ (the origin is excluded). ```fig z-finite ``` ## Example 10.7 — $b^{|n|}$ $x[n]=b^{|n|}=b^nu[n]+b^{-n}u[-n-1]$ with $b>0$. - $0<b<1$: the right-sided part needs $|z|>b$ and the left-sided part $|z|<\frac1b$, so the ROC is $b<|z|<\frac1b$. - $b>1$: the conditions $|z|>b$ and $|z|<\frac1b$ cannot both hold, so **no ROC exists**. ```fig z-bn ``` > A formal algebraic manipulation can exist, but without a non-empty ROC there is no z-transform. ## Properties 7–9 — rational transforms - **Property 7.** If $X(z)$ is rational, its ROC is bounded by poles or extends to 0 and/or $\infty$. - **Property 8.** Rational and right-sided: the ROC is **outside the outermost pole** (outside the circle through the finite pole of largest magnitude). If the sequence is causal, the ROC also includes $\infty$. - **Property 9.** Rational and left-sided: the ROC is **inside the innermost non-zero pole**; it may include 0, and does if the sequence is anticausal. Poles draw the possible circular boundaries; the ROC chooses the side: exterior → right-sided, interior → left-sided, annulus → two-sided. ## Example 10.8 — three possible ROCs

X(z)=\frac1{\left(1-\frac12z^{-1}\right)\left(1-2z^{-1}\right)},\qquad\text{poles at }z=\tfrac12,\ 2 .

The possible ROCs are $|z|>2$ (right-sided), $\frac12<|z|<2$ (two-sided) and $|z|<\frac12$ (left-sided). Only the middle annulus contains the unit circle, so only that sequence has a DTFT. ```fig z-ex108 ``` # Part III — the inverse z-transform ## The contour formula Recall $X(re^{j\omega})=\mathcal F\{x[n]r^{-n}\}$. If the circle $|z|=r$ lies in the ROC, apply the inverse DTFT and multiply by $r^n$:

x[n]r^{-n}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{2\pi}X(re^{j\omega})e^{j\omega n}d\omega\quad\Rightarrow\quad x[n]=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{2\pi}X(re^{j\omega})(re^{j\omega})^nd\omega .

With $z=re^{j\omega}$ and $dz=jre^{j\omega}d\omega=jz\,d\omega$:

\boxed{,x[n]=\frac1{2\pi j}\oint_CX(z),z^{n-1},dz,}

where $C$ is any **counter-clockwise** closed contour lying entirely within the ROC (and encircling the origin). It is evaluated with residues. ## Partial fractions ### Example 10.9 — an exterior ROC

X(z)=\frac{3-\frac56z^{-1}}{\left(1-\frac14z^{-1}\right)\left(1-\frac13z^{-1}\right)},\qquad|z|>\tfrac13 .

Partial fractions give $X(z)=\dfrac1{1-\frac14z^{-1}}+\dfrac2{1-\frac13z^{-1}}$. The ROC is outside the outermost pole, so both terms are right-sided:

x[n]=\left(\tfrac14\right)^nu[n]+2\left(\tfrac13\right)^nu[n].

> **Main idea:** the partial fractions do not change; the ROC decides how each term is interpreted. ### Examples 10.10 and 10.11 — same algebra, different sequences Keep the same expansion. - **ROC $\frac14<|z|<\frac13$** (10.10): the pole at $\frac14$ is inside the ring's inner edge (right-sided term); the pole at $\frac13$ is outside it (left-sided term): $$x[n]=\left(\tfrac14\right)^nu[n]-2\left(\tfrac13\right)^nu[-n-1].$$ - **ROC $|z|<\frac14$** (10.11): both terms are left-sided: $$x[n]=-\left(\tfrac14\right)^nu[-n-1]-2\left(\tfrac13\right)^nu[-n-1].$$ ## Power-series inversion Rewrite $X(z)$ as a power series that is valid in the ROC and match it with $X(z)=\sum_nx[n]z^{-n}$. **The coefficient of $z^{-n}$ is $x[n]$**; positive powers of $z$ represent negative-time samples. **Example 10.12.** $$\begin{aligned}&X(z)=4z^2+2+3z^{-1}\\[6pt]&\Rightarrow x[n]=4\delta[n+2]+2\delta[n]+3\delta[n-1]\end{aligned}$$ **Example 10.13.** $X(z)=\dfrac1{1-az^{-1}}$. - If $|z|>|a|$ ($|az^{-1}|<1$): $X=1+az^{-1}+a^2z^{-2}+\cdots\Rightarrow x[n]=a^nu[n]$. - If $|z|<|a|$ ($|z/a|<1$): $X=\dfrac{-z/a}{1-z/a}=-a^{-1}z-a^{-2}z^2-a^{-3}z^3-\cdots\Rightarrow x[n]=-a^nu[-n-1]$. **Example 10.14.** $X(z)=\log(1+az^{-1})$, $|z|>|a|$. From the geometric series $\frac1{1+t}=1-t+t^2-t^3+\cdots$ ($|t|<1$), integrate from 0 to $v$: $$\begin{aligned}&\log(1+v)\\[6pt]&= \int_0^v\frac{dt}{1+t}\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}v^n}{n},\qquad|v|<1 . \end{aligned}$$ Set $v=az^{-1}$; since $|z|>|a|$, $|az^{-1}|<1$:

X(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}a^n}{n}z^{-n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x[n]=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n+1}\dfrac{a^n}n,&n\ge1\0,&n\le0\end{cases}

x_{(k)}[n]=\begin{cases}x[n/k],&n\text{ a multiple of }k\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\leftrightarrow\qquad X(z^k),\qquad\text{ROC}=R^{1/k}.

Just as with Laplace, many system properties are visible directly in the transform domain. ## Causality from the ROC > A discrete-time LTI system is **causal iff the ROC of $H(z)$ is the exterior of a circle and includes $\infty$**. **Example 10.20.** $H(z)=\dfrac{z^3-2z^2+z}{z^2+\frac14z+\frac18}$ is **not causal**: written in powers of $z$, the numerator degree exceeds the denominator degree, so $H(z)$ blows up as $z\to\infty$. **Example 10.21.** $H(z)=\dfrac1{1-\frac12z^{-1}}+\dfrac1{1-2z^{-1}}$ with $|z|>2$: the ROC is outside the outermost pole and includes $\infty$, so the system **is causal**. ## Stability from the ROC > An LTI system is **stable iff the ROC of $H(z)$ includes the unit circle**. - **Example 10.22:** the same $H(z)$ as Example 10.21 with ROC $|z|>2$ is **unstable** (the unit circle is not in the ROC). - **Example 10.23:** a causal first-order system $H(z)=\frac1{1-az^{-1}}$ is stable iff $|a|<1$. - **Example 10.24:** $H(z)=\dfrac1{1-2r\cos\theta\,z^{-1}+r^2z^{-2}}$ has poles at $re^{\pm j\theta}$, so a causal system is stable iff $r<1$. ```fig z-causal z-poles-circle z-interactive-poles ``` **Causal and stable** ⇔ all poles inside the unit circle (for rational $H$). ## Example 10.25 — difference equations become algebra

y[n]-\tfrac12y[n-1]=x[n]+\tfrac13x[n-1].

y[n]=\frac83u[n]-\frac53\left(\frac12\right)^nu[n].

Check for pole–zero cancellation (Example 10.6). - Logarithms and other analytic functions: use the Taylor series, or $n\,x[n]\leftrightarrow-z\frac{dX}{dz}$. - Inverse contour formula: $x[n]=\frac1{2\pi j}\oint X(z)z^{n-1}dz$ from inverting $\mathcal F\{x[n]r^{-n}\}$ with $d\omega=\frac{dz}{jz}$.
