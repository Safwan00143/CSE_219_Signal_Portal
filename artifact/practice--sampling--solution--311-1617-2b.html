(i) Why band-limit before sampling (anti-aliasing filter). The proof assumes G(f)=0G(f)=0G(f)=0 for ∣f∣>B|f|>B∣f∣>B. Real signals are time-limited, so they are never strictly band-limited: their spectra have tails extending to infinity. Without a filter, the tails above fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 fold back into the baseband as false low frequencies (aliasing), and nothing after sampling can remove them. A low-pass filter cutting off at the essential bandwidth B<fs/2B<f_s/2B<fs​/2 before the sampler discards those components deliberately, so nothing folds back. The loss of the weak high-frequency tail is far less harmful than the aliasing it prevents.

(ii) Why sample faster than Nyquist. At exactly fs=2Bf_s=2Bfs​=2B the spectral copies touch at f=±Bf=\pm Bf=±B, with no gap between them. Separating them would need an ideal brick-wall low-pass filter, which has a sinc impulse response and is non-causal and unrealisable. Real filters (and the anti-aliasing filter in (i)) have a finite transition band. Sampling faster, typically 10–25% above Nyquist, opens a guard band between BBB and fs−Bf_s-Bfs​−B in which a practical filter can roll off. Example: telephone speech has B=3.4B=3.4B=3.4 kHz but is sampled at 888 kHz, not 6.86.86.8 kHz.

Practical issues in reconstruction (2013-14 Q2(b)). The ideal reconstruction filter is unrealisable, so a zero-order hold followed by a smoothing filter is used. The ZOH's sinc⁡\operatorname{sinc}sinc response droops inside the passband (often compensated by equalisation) and leaks image copies outside it, which the smoothing filter must suppress. Oversampling makes both tasks easier.
