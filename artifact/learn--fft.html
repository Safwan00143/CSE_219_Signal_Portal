Topic 09·Ashraf Sir · Lectures 3–4

Fast Fourier transform

Radix-2 decimation in time and in frequency, butterflies, bit reversal and cost.

3 past questions on this topic

Recap: four views of the DFT

  • Periodic, view 1: the DFT evaluates the Fourier series coefficients of a sampled periodic signal.
  • Periodic, view 2: it gives the NNN unique values of the Fourier series of a discrete-time periodic signal.
  • Aperiodic, view 1: it gives samples of the continuous-time Fourier transform of a sampled aperiodic signal.
  • Aperiodic, view 2: it gives samples of the DTFT of a finite-length sequence.

(And, as we will see in Sampling, if the original continuous signal is band-limited we can recover it from the DFT.)

Part I — the need for speed

Naive evaluation of the DFT

X[k]=∑n=0N−1x[n]WNkn,WN=e−j2πN.X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]W_N^{kn},\qquad W_N=e^{-j\frac{2\pi}N}.X[k]=n=0∑N−1​x[n]WNkn​,WN​=e−jN2π​.

For each of the NNN values of kkk we need NNN complex multiplications and N−1N-1N−1 complex additions. All NNN outputs therefore cost

N2 multiplications,N(N−1) additions⇒O(N2).N^2\ \text{multiplications},\qquad N(N-1)\ \text{additions}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad O(N^2).N2 multiplications,N(N−1) additions⇒O(N2).

Why that is a problem. One second of audio sampled at 44.1 kHz has N=44 100N=44\,100N=44100. The naive DFT needs about N2≈2×109N^2\approx2\times10^9N2≈2×109 operations, which makes spectral analysis impossibly slow.

A brief history

  • 1805, Carl Friedrich Gauss developed the radix-2 algorithm to interpolate asteroid orbits, more than a decade before Fourier's work on harmonic analysis. He left it as an unpublished note.
  • 1965, James Cooley and John Tukey independently rediscovered it at IBM and Princeton. Their landmark paper launched modern digital signal processing.

Many variants exist; we study the Cooley–Tukey FFT.

The idea

The Cooley–Tukey FFT is an O(Nlog⁡N)O(N\log N)O(NlogN) algorithm that exploits two facts about the twiddle factor:

WNk+N=WNk  (periodicity),WNk+N/2=WNke−jπ=−WNk  (symmetry),WN2=WN/2.W_N^{k+N}=W_N^k\ \ (\text{periodicity}),\qquad W_N^{k+N/2}=W_N^ke^{-j\pi}=-W_N^k\ \ (\text{symmetry}),\qquad W_N^2=W_{N/2}.WNk+N​=WNk​  (periodicity),WNk+N/2​=WNk​e−jπ=−WNk​  (symmetry),WN2​=WN/2​.

The radix-2 algorithm successively splits an NNN-point DFT into two N2\frac N22N​-point DFTs. It assumes NNN is a power of 2 (otherwise zero-pad). There are two variants: decimation in time (DIT) and decimation in frequency (DIF).

Part II — radix-2 decimation in time

Split the input into even and odd samples

X[k]=∑n=0N−1x[n]WNkn=∑r=0N/2−1x[2r]WN2rk+∑r=0N/2−1x[2r+1]WN(2r+1)k.\begin{aligned}&X[k]\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]W_N^{kn}\\[6pt]&= \sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1}x[2r]W_N^{2rk}+\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1}x[2r+1]W_N^{(2r+1)k}.\end{aligned}​X[k]=n=0∑N−1​x[n]WNkn​=r=0∑N/2−1​x[2r]WN2rk​+r=0∑N/2−1​x[2r+1]WN(2r+1)k​.​

Since

WN2rk=e−j2πN2rk=e−j2πN/2rk=WN/2rk\begin{aligned}&W_N^{2rk}\\[6pt]&= e^{-j\frac{2\pi}N2rk}\\[6pt]&= e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N/2}rk}\\[6pt]&= W_{N/2}^{rk}\end{aligned}​WN2rk​=e−jN2π​2rk=e−jN/22π​rk=WN/2rk​​

X[k]=∑r=0N/2−1x[2r]WN/2rk⏟N2-point DFT of evens+WNk∑r=0N/2−1x[2r+1]WN/2rk⏟N2-point DFT of odds.X[k]=\underbrace{\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1}x[2r]W_{N/2}^{rk}}_{\frac N2\text{-point DFT of evens}}+W_N^k\underbrace{\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1}x[2r+1]W_{N/2}^{rk}}_{\frac N2\text{-point DFT of odds}} .X[k]=2N​-point DFT of evensr=0∑N/2−1​x[2r]WN/2rk​​​+WNk​2N​-point DFT of oddsr=0∑N/2−1​x[2r+1]WN/2rk​​​.

So an NNN-point DFT can be computed from two N2\frac N22N​-point DFTs. Define

G[k]=DFTN/2{x[2r]},H[k]=DFTN/2{x[2r+1]}.G[k]=\text{DFT}_{N/2}\{x[2r]\},\qquad H[k]=\text{DFT}_{N/2}\{x[2r+1]\}.G[k]=DFTN/2​{x[2r]},H[k]=DFTN/2​{x[2r+1]}.

From N2\frac N22N​ points back to NNN points

GGG and HHH are defined for 0≤k<N20\le k<\frac N20≤k<2N​, but as N2\frac N22N​-point DFTs they are N2\frac N22N​-periodic: G[k+N2]=G[k]G[k+\frac N2]=G[k]G[k+2N​]=G[k], H[k+N2]=H[k]H[k+\frac N2]=H[k]H[k+2N​]=H[k]. Copying the first half to the second, X[k]=G[k]+WNkH[k]X[k]=G[k]+W_N^kH[k]X[k]=G[k]+WNk​H[k] for all kkk.

We can do better. For the second half use the symmetry WNk+N/2=−WNkW_N^{k+N/2}=-W_N^kWNk+N/2​=−WNk​:

X[k+N2]=G[k]+WNk+N/2H[k]=G[k]−WNkH[k].\begin{aligned}&X[k+\tfrac N2]\\[6pt]&= G[k]+W_N^{k+N/2}H[k]\\[6pt]&= G[k]-W_N^kH[k].\end{aligned}​X[k+2N​]=G[k]+WNk+N/2​H[k]=G[k]−WNk​H[k].​

Radix-2 DIT butterfly. For 0≤k<N20\le k<\frac N20≤k<2N​: X[k]=G[k]+WNkH[k],X[k+N2]=G[k]−WNkH[k].X[k]=G[k]+W_N^kH[k],\qquad X[k+\tfrac N2]=G[k]-W_N^kH[k].X[k]=G[k]+WNk​H[k],X[k+2N​]=G[k]−WNk​H[k].

We compute each half-size DFT once, then combine with one addition, one subtraction and a single complex multiplication (the twiddle) per pair of outputs.

Butterflies by size

N=2N=2N=2. The fundamental 2-point DFT, with W20=1W_2^0=1W20​=1:

X[0]=x[0]+x[1],X[1]=x[0]−x[1].X[0]=x[0]+x[1],\qquad X[1]=x[0]-x[1].X[0]=x[0]+x[1],X[1]=x[0]−x[1].

N=4N=4N=4. Two 2-point DFTs, GGG from {x[0],x[2]}\{x[0],x[2]\}{x[0],x[2]} and HHH from {x[1],x[3]}\{x[1],x[3]\}{x[1],x[3]}, combined with W4kW_4^kW4k​:

X[0]=G[0]+W40H[0],X[1]=G[1]+W41H[1],X[2]=G[0]−W40H[0],X[3]=G[1]−W41H[1].X[0]=G[0]+W_4^0H[0],\quad X[1]=G[1]+W_4^1H[1],\quad X[2]=G[0]-W_4^0H[0],\quad X[3]=G[1]-W_4^1H[1].X[0]=G[0]+W40​H[0],X[1]=G[1]+W41​H[1],X[2]=G[0]−W40​H[0],X[3]=G[1]−W41​H[1].

N=8N=8N=8. First split into two 4-point DFTs of the evens and odds and combine with X[k]=G[k]+W8kH[k]X[k]=G[k]+W_8^kH[k]X[k]=G[k]+W8k​H[k], X[k+4]=G[k]−W8kH[k]X[k+4]=G[k]-W_8^kH[k]X[k+4]=G[k]−W8k​H[k] for k=0,…,3k=0,\dots,3k=0,…,3. Expanding every 4- and 2-point DFT gives the complete structure:

Understanding bit-reversed order

In DIT the inputs appear in bit-reversed order, because the sequence is divided again and again into even- and odd-indexed halves. For N=8N=8N=8:

  1. Stage 1: even (0,2,4,6)(0,2,4,6)(0,2,4,6), odd (1,3,5,7)(1,3,5,7)(1,3,5,7).
  2. Stage 2: even-even (0,4)(0,4)(0,4), odd-even (2,6)(2,6)(2,6), even-odd (1,5)(1,5)(1,5), odd-odd (3,7)(3,7)(3,7).
  3. Stage 3: single elements x[0],x[4],x[2],x[6],x[1],x[5],x[3],x[7]x[0],x[4],x[2],x[6],x[1],x[5],x[3],x[7]x[0],x[4],x[2],x[6],x[1],x[5],x[3],x[7].

Putting evens before odds sorts by the rightmost bit; putting even-even before odd-even sorts by the second rightmost bit, and so on. That is the same as sorting by the bit-reversed index.

The price is the bit-reversed ordering at one end. The FFT computes **exactly** the same numbers as the DFT; it is not an approximation. # Part III — radix-2 decimation in frequency ## Split the sum into first and second halves Instead of separating even and odd time indices, DIF splits $n=0,\dots,N-1$ into the first and second halves:

X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}x[n]W_N^{kn}+\sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}x[n+\tfrac N2]W_N^{k(n+N/2)} .

Since $W_N^{kN/2}=e^{-j\pi k}=(-1)^k$:

X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\Big(x[n]+(-1)^kx[n+\tfrac N2]\Big)W_N^{kn}.

Now **decimate in frequency**, evaluating even and odd $k$ separately. - **Even $k=2m$:** $(-1)^{2m}=1$ and $W_N^{2mn}=W_{N/2}^{mn}$: $$\begin{aligned}&X[2m]\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\underbrace{\big(x[n]+x[n+\tfrac N2]\big)}_{a(n)}W_{N/2}^{mn}\\[6pt]&= \text{DFT}_{N/2}\{a(n)\}.\end{aligned}$$ - **Odd $k=2m+1$:** $(-1)^{2m+1}=-1$ and $W_N^{(2m+1)n}=W_N^nW_{N/2}^{mn}$: $$\begin{aligned}&X[2m+1]\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1}\underbrace{\big(x[n]-x[n+\tfrac N2]\big)W_N^n}_{b(n)}W_{N/2}^{mn}\\[6pt]&= \text{DFT}_{N/2}\{b(n)\}.\end{aligned}$$ > **DIF butterfly:** $a(n)=x[n]+x[n+\frac N2]$ and $b(n)=\big(x[n]-x[n+\frac N2]\big)W_N^n$. The addition and subtraction come **before** the twiddle multiplication; in DIT the twiddle comes first. Repeat on each half until 2-point DFTs remain. The full 8-point DIF structure has **natural-order inputs and bit-reversed outputs**: $X[0],X[4],X[2],X[6],X[1],X[5],X[3],X[7]$. ```fig fft-dif-8 ``` **DIT vs DIF at a glance:** | | DIT | DIF | |---|---|---| | splits | input by even / odd index | input into first / second half | | decimates | time | frequency (even / odd outputs) | | twiddle | before the add/subtract | after the subtract | | input order | bit-reversed | natural | | output order | natural | bit-reversed | | cost | $\frac N2\log_2N$ mults | $\frac N2\log_2N$ mults | ## Why zero-pad? Radix-2 FFTs need $N=2^m$ exactly. Real data often has $M$ samples with $M$ not a power of 2; stuck at $M$ samples we would be back to the slow $O(M^2)$ DFT. **Solution:** append zeros up to the next power of two, $N=2^{\lceil\log_2M\rceil}$. - Bonus: zero-padding interpolates the spectrum, giving a smoother picture without altering the true frequency content $X(e^{j\omega})$. - Caution: for a periodic $x[n]$, zero-padding changes the period and shape of the periodic signal. Can we avoid padding? Yes, next. # Part IV — advanced FFT algorithms ## Radix-2 as a matrix Reshape the 1-D input into a 2-D array and do DFTs along rows and columns. 1. Arrange $x[n]$ in a $2\times\frac N2$ matrix in **column-major** order, $n=2n_2+n_1$: row $n_1=0$ holds the even samples $x[0],x[2],x[4],\dots$; row $n_1=1$ holds the odd samples $x[1],x[3],x[5],\dots$ 2. **First DFTs:** an $\frac N2$-point DFT along each row gives $G[k]$ (row 0) and $H[k]$ (row 1). 3. **Twiddles:** multiply row $n_1$ by $W_N^{n_1k}$, so row 1 becomes $H[0]W_N^0,\ H[1]W_N^1,\ H[2]W_N^2,\dots$ 4. **Second DFTs:** a 2-point DFT down each column. This is exactly the butterfly. 5. Row 0 now holds $X[0\dots\frac N2-1]$ and row 1 holds $X[\frac N2\dots N-1]$: read the output in **row-major** order, $k=\frac N2k_1+k_2$: $$X\big[\tfrac N2k_1+k_2\big]=G[k_2]+W_2^{k_1}W_N^{k_2}H[k_2].$$ > The Cooley–Tukey algorithm is: arrange numbers on a matrix, perform DFTs on the rows and columns. ## Radix 3 For $N=3N_2$, split by index modulo 3:

X[k]=\sum_rx[3r]W_{N/3}^{rk}+W_N^k\sum_rx[3r+1]W_{N/3}^{rk}+W_N^{2k}\sum_rx[3r+2]W_{N/3}^{rk}.

With $G_m[k]=\sum_rx[3r+m]W_{N/3}^{rk}$ for $m=0,1,2$:

\begin{aligned} X[k]&=G_0[k]+(W_N^kG_1[k])+(W_N^{2k}G_2[k])\ X[k+\tfrac N3]&=G_0[k]+W_3^1(W_N^kG_1[k])+W_3^2(W_N^{2k}G_2[k])\ X[k+\tfrac{2N}3]&=G_0[k]+W_3^2(W_N^kG_1[k])+W_3^4(W_N^{2k}G_2[k]) \end{aligned}

These three equations are themselves a 3-point DFT. In matrix form: load $x$ into a $3\times\frac N3$ array column-major ($n=3n_2+n_1$), take $\frac N3$-point DFTs along rows, multiply by $W_N^{n_1k_2}$, take 3-point DFTs down columns, read row-major ($k=\frac N3k_1+k_2$). ## General Cooley–Tukey: $N=N_1N_2$ Index the input as an $N_1\times N_2$ matrix column-major, $n=N_1n_2+n_1$, and the output row-major, $k=N_2k_1+k_2$. Expand the exponent:

(N_1n_2+n_1)(N_2k_1+k_2)=Nn_2k_1+N_1n_2k_2+N_2n_1k_1+n_1k_2 .

- $W_N^{Nn_2k_1}=1$: a full turn, so this term vanishes. - $W_N^{N_1n_2k_2}=W_{N_2}^{n_2k_2}$ and $W_N^{N_2n_1k_1}=W_{N_1}^{n_1k_1}$ (since $W_N^{N_1m}=e^{-j2\pi m/N_2}$). - $W_N^{n_1k_2}$ stays as it is.

\boxed{,X[N_2k_1+k_2]=\sum_{n_1=0}^{N_1-1}\left[W_N^{n_1k_2}\left(\sum_{n_2=0}^{N_2-1}x[N_1n_2+n_1]W_{N_2}^{n_2k_2}\right)\right]W_{N_1}^{n_1k_1},}

The inner sum is an $N_2$-point DFT along each row; $W_N^{n_1k_2}$ is the twiddle; the outer sum is an $N_1$-point DFT down each column. ### Bailey's (four-step) FFT 1. Arrange $x[n]$ in an $N_1\times N_2$ matrix (column-major). 2. Compute the $N_2$-point DFT of each row. 3. Multiply entry $(n_1,k_2)$ by $W_N^{n_1k_2}$. 4. Compute the $N_1$-point DFT of each column, and read row-major. Apply the same algorithm recursively to the smaller DFTs. If $N=p_1^{a_1}p_2^{a_2}\cdots$, factor until only small primes remain: $O(N\log N)$ for any composite $N$ (powers of 2, powers of 3, mixed radices). # Part V — fast convolution ## The filtering recipe, now with the FFT From the DFT topic: LTI filtering is linear convolution ($L\cdot M$ multiplications directly); multiplying DFTs gives circular convolution; padding to $N\ge L+M-1$ makes circular equal linear. The transform was the bottleneck. Replace every DFT by an FFT. **Fast convolution** (input length $L$, filter length $M$): 1. Choose $N\ge L+M-1$, the next power of 2. 2. Zero-pad $x$ and $h$ to length $N$. 3. $X[k]=\text{FFT}\{x\}$ and $H[k]=\text{FFT}\{h\}$. 4. Multiply bin by bin: $Y[k]=X[k]H[k]$. 5. $y[n]=\text{IFFT}\{Y\}$; keep the first $L+M-1$ samples. Padding beyond $L+M-1$ is harmless: it only appends trailing zeros to $y$. ## How much faster? - Direct linear convolution: $O(L\cdot M)$; with $L\approx M\approx N/2$, roughly $O(N^2)$. - Fast convolution: two forward FFTs, one element-wise product $O(N)$, one inverse FFT: $O(N\log N)$ in total. **Real-world magnitude.** A one-second reverb ($M=44\,100$) on one second of audio ($L=44\,100$): - naive: $L\times M\approx1.94\times10^9$ operations; - fast: pad to $N=131\,072=2^{17}$; $3\times N\log_2N\approx6.7\times10^6$ operations. About a **300× speed-up**, of purely algorithmic origin. This is what makes real-time, high-fidelity audio processing possible. # Part VI — FFT for prime lengths: Bluestein's algorithm ## The problem Cooley–Tukey gives $O(N\log N)$ for any composite $N$. But if $N$ is a large prime (say $N=1\,000\,003$), then $N_1=1$ and Cooley–Tukey falls back to $O(N^2)$. ## The chirp-z identity

(k-n)^2=k^2-2kn+n^2\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad kn=\frac{k^2+n^2-(k-n)^2}{2},

so $$\begin{aligned}&W_N^{kn}\\[6pt]&= W_{2N}^{2kn}\\[6pt]&= W_{2N}^{k^2}\,W_{2N}^{n^2}\,W_{2N}^{-(k-n)^2}.\end{aligned}$$ ## The DFT is a convolution $$\begin{aligned}&X[k]\\[6pt]&= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]W_N^{kn}\\[6pt]&= W_{2N}^{k^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\underbrace{x[n]W_{2N}^{n^2}}_{a[n]}\ \underbrace{W_{2N}^{-(k-n)^2}}_{b[k-n]}\\[6pt]&= W_{2N}^{k^2}\,(a*b)[k],\end{aligned}$$ with $a[n]=x[n]W_{2N}^{n^2}$ for $n\in[0,N-1]$ and the **chirp** $b[n]=W_{2N}^{-n^2}$ for $n\in[-(N-1),N-1]$. The sum is exactly a **linear convolution**, which we can compute fast. ## Padding the chirp without breaking the wrap The convolution evaluates $b[k-n]$ with $k-n$ running from $k-(N-1)$ to $k$, which can be negative. In a length-$M$ circular buffer a negative index $n$ lands at $((n))_M=M+n$: the DFT topic's wrap, now used **on purpose**. - Positive indices of $b$ go at the start of the size-$M$ array, negative indices at the end, with zeros between so they never overlap: $M\ge2N-1$. - Choose $M$ a power of 2, so radix-2 FFTs apply. The prime $N$ never touches the transform length. ```fig fft-bluestein ``` **Bluestein's FFT:** 1. Form $a[n]=x[n]W_{2N}^{n^2}$ and the wrapped chirp $b[((n))_M]$, both of size $M=2^{\lceil\log_2(2N-1)\rceil}$. 2. Compute $A=\text{FFT}(a,M)$ and $B=\text{FFT}(b,M)$. 3. Multiply: $Y[k]=A[k]B[k]$. 4. Invert: $y[n]=\text{IFFT}(Y,M)$. 5. Multiply by the chirp: $X[k]=y[k]\,W_{2N}^{k^2}$ for $k=0,\dots,N-1$. > **Summary.** Every $N$ has an $O(N\log N)$ transform: factor it if you can (Cooley–Tukey), chirp it if you cannot (Bluestein). And with it comes the fastest known way to filter: fast convolution. ## Exam pointers - Derive the DIT butterfly in three lines: split by parity, use $W_N^2=W_{N/2}$, then $W_N^{k+N/2}=-W_N^k$. - Derive the DIF split: first/second half, $W_N^{kN/2}=(-1)^k$, then even/odd $k$. - Draw flow graphs with every twiddle labelled and the bit-reversed end marked; know the bit-reversal table. - Complexity: give both counts, $N^2$ against $\frac N2\log_2N$, the recurrence $M(N)=2M(N/2)+N/2$, and a numeric example.
