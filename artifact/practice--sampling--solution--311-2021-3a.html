Justification.

  • A practical signal exists for a finite time. A time-limited signal cannot be band-limited: its spectrum has tails extending to infinite frequency. The rect pulse, for example, has a sinc spectrum.
  • The sampling theorem guarantees perfect recovery only for band-limited signals with fs≥2Bf_s\ge2Bfs​≥2B. Here B=∞B=\inftyB=∞, so no finite fsf_sfs​ meets the condition.
  • Increasing fsf_sfs​ pushes the spectral copies further apart and makes the overlap smaller, but it never reaches exactly zero.

Phenomenon: aliasing. In Gˉ(f)=1Ts∑G(f−nfs)\bar G(f)=\frac1{T_s}\sum G(f-nf_s)Gˉ(f)=Ts​1​∑G(f−nfs​), the tails of the copies at ±fs\pm f_s±fs​ spill into ∣f∣<fs/2|f|<f_s/2∣f∣<fs​/2.

How it distorts.

  • The reconstruction low-pass filter (cutoff fs/2f_s/2fs​/2) passes the baseband plus the folded tails of the neighbouring copies.
  • Components above fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 are lost, and they reappear mirrored about fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 as false lower frequencies.
  • The recovered waveform is therefore wrong in its high-frequency detail, and the error cannot be filtered out afterwards.

Minimising it.

  1. Anti-aliasing low-pass filter before the sampler, cutting off at the essential bandwidth BBB (e.g. 3.4 kHz for speech). The truncated tail is lost, but nothing folds back.
  2. Sample above the Nyquist rate (fs>2Bf_s>2Bfs​>2B) to leave guard bands, so realisable filters work.
  3. Choose BBB so the discarded energy is negligible, e.g. 99% essential bandwidth.

What remains is a small, controlled error instead of uncontrolled aliasing.
