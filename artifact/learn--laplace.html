Part I — the Laplace transform

Why generalise the Fourier transform?

A broad class of signals can be written as sums of complex exponentials ejωte^{j\omega t}ejωt, and these are eigenfunctions of LTI systems: x(t)=ejωtx(t)=e^{j\omega t}x(t)=ejωt gives y(t)=H(jω)ejωty(t)=H(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}y(t)=H(jω)ejωt. That is how we expressed both input and output of an LTI system as sums of exponentials.

But the eigenfunction property holds for any complex s=σ+jωs=\sigma+j\omegas=σ+jω, not just s=jωs=j\omegas=jω. Using it leads to the Laplace transform, which:

  • analyses broad classes of signals and unstable systems for which the Fourier transform does not exist;
  • gives deeper insight into stability and instability;
  • keeps powerful algebraic properties, especially for feedback systems.

Connection to the Fourier transform

On the imaginary axis (s=jωs=j\omegas=jω) the Laplace transform is the Fourier transform:

X(jω)=∫x(t)e−jωtdt=F{x(t)}\begin{aligned}&X(j\omega)\\[6pt]&= \int x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt\\[6pt]&= \mathcal F\{x(t)\}\end{aligned}​X(jω)=∫x(t)e−jωtdt=F{x(t)}​

For general sss:

X(σ+jω)=∫−∞∞[x(t)e−σt]e−jωtdt=F{x(t)e−σt}.\begin{aligned}&X(\sigma+j\omega)\\[6pt]&= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\big]e^{-j\omega t}dt\\[6pt]&= \mathcal F\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}.\end{aligned}​X(σ+jω)=∫−∞∞​[x(t)e−σt]e−jωtdt=F{x(t)e−σt}.​

Key insight. The Laplace transform of x(t)x(t)x(t) is the Fourier transform of x(t)e−σtx(t)e^{-\sigma t}x(t)e−σt. The real exponential weight e−σte^{-\sigma t}e−σt can force a signal into convergence even when the Fourier transform of x(t)x(t)x(t) alone does not exist.

The s-plane and the region of convergence

X(s)X(s)X(s) depends on the complex variable s=σ+jωs=\sigma+j\omegas=σ+jω, so we picture it on the s-plane: horizontal axis σ=Re⁡{s}\sigma=\operatorname{Re}\{s\}σ=Re{s}, vertical axis jωj\omegajω.

The region of convergence (ROC) is the set of sss for which the integral converges to a finite value. Unlike for the Fourier transform, the ROC is an essential part of the Laplace transform: Examples 9.1 and 9.2 have the same algebraic X(s)X(s)X(s) and different ROCs, and they are different signals.

The jωj\omegajω axis (σ=0\sigma=0σ=0) corresponds to the Fourier transform. If the ROC includes it, the Fourier transform of the signal converges.

Convergence depends only on $\sigma$, so if one point on a vertical line converges, the whole line does. **Property 2.** For rational transforms, **the ROC contains no poles**: $X(s)$ is infinite at a pole, so the integral diverges there. ## Property 3 — finite-duration signals If $x(t)$ has finite duration and is absolutely integrable, **the ROC is the entire s-plane**. *Why:* let $x(t)\neq0$ only on $T_1<t<T_2$. On a finite interval $e^{-\sigma t}$ is bounded for any finite $\sigma$: its largest value is $e^{-\sigma T_1}$ if $\sigma>0$ and $e^{-\sigma T_2}$ if $\sigma<0$. Calling that point $T_{\max}$,

Change variables: $s=\sigma+j\omega$ with $\sigma$ fixed, so $ds=j\,d\omega$, and $\omega:-\infty\to\infty$ becomes $s:\sigma-j\infty\to\sigma+j\infty$.

\boxed{,x(t)=\frac1{2\pi j}\int_{\sigma-j\infty}^{\sigma+j\infty}X(s),e^{st},ds,}

$\sigma$ can be chosen anywhere **within the ROC**; otherwise $X(s)$ is not defined. ## The Bromwich integral This is the **Bromwich integral** (Mellin inversion formula): a contour integral along the vertical line $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}=\sigma$. In practice the contour is closed with an infinite semicircle, to the left for $t>0$ (enclosing the poles to the left) or to the right for $t<0$, and evaluated with the residue theorem, $\oint_Cf(s)ds=2\pi j\sum\text{residues inside }C$. This is mathematically demanding, so we rarely do it by hand. **Numerical approximation.** Replace the integral over $\omega$ by a Riemann sum with small steps $\Delta\omega$:

x(t)\approx\frac{\Delta\omega}{2\pi}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(\sigma+jk\Delta\omega)e^{(\sigma+jk\Delta\omega)t},

## Integration in time

\int_{-\infty}^{t}x(\tau),d\tau\ \leftrightarrow\ \frac1sX(s),\qquad\text{ROC contains }R\cap{\operatorname{Re}{s}>0}.

*Proof:* $\int_{-\infty}^tx(\tau)d\tau=x(t)*u(t)$, and $u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac1s$ with $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>0$. *Example:* $\int_{-\infty}^tu(\tau)d\tau=t\,u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac1{s^2}$, $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>0$. ## The transform-domain view For an LTI system with impulse response $h(t)$,

Y(s)=H(s)X(s),\qquad H(s)=\mathcal L{h(t)}\ \ (\text{the system function}).

Convolution in time becomes multiplication in $s$. For $x(t)=e^{st}$, $y(t)=H(s)e^{st}$ whenever $s$ is in the ROC of $H$. If the ROC includes the $j\omega$ axis, $H(j\omega)$ is the frequency response. This is why poles, zeros and the ROC are so valuable: they reveal causality, stability and the shape of the response, often just by inspecting $H(s)$. ## Causality from the ROC - A causal system has $h(t)=0$ for $t<0$ (right-sided), so the **ROC of $H(s)$ is a right half-plane**. - If $H(s)$ is rational, causality ⇔ the ROC is to the **right of the rightmost pole**. **Example 9.17.** $$h(t)=e^{-t}u(t)\Rightarrow H(s)=\frac1{s+1},$$ $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>-1$: right-sided, so causal. **Example 9.18.** $$h(t)=e^{-|t|}\Rightarrow H(s)=\frac{-2}{s^2-1},$$ $-1<\operatorname{Re}\{s\}<1$: two-sided, so not causal. ## Stability from the ROC > An LTI system is **stable iff the ROC of $H(s)$ includes the entire $j\omega$ axis**. (Stability means $\int|h|<\infty$, which is exactly convergence of the transform at $\sigma=0$.) **Example 9.20.** $H(s)=\dfrac{s-1}{(s+1)(s-2)}$ has poles at $-1$ and $2$, hence three possible ROCs: - $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>2$: causal but unstable; - $-1<\operatorname{Re}\{s\}<2$: non-causal but stable; - $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}<-1$: anticausal and unstable. Only the middle ROC contains the $j\omega$ axis. ```fig lap-stability ``` ## Causal + stable: the pole test > A **causal** system with rational $H(s)$ is stable **iff all poles of $H(s)$ lie in the left half-plane**. **Example 9.21.** $H(s)=\frac1{s-2}$ with the causal ROC $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>2$: the pole is in the right half-plane, so the system is unstable. ```fig lap-causal-stable ``` # Part VI — differential equations ## The system function from a differential equation Often we are given a differential equation instead of $h(t)$. Take Laplace transforms (zero initial conditions); every derivative becomes a factor of $s$: $$\begin{aligned}&\frac{dy}{dt}+3y(t)=x(t)\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal L\ }\ sY(s)+3Y(s)=X(s)\ \\[6pt]&\Rightarrow \ H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac1{s+3}.\end{aligned}$$ We can then analyse the system with $H(s)$, or find $h(t)$ by inverting it, being careful about the ROC. **Example 9.23.** The same equation gives two possible impulse responses:

h(t)=e^{-3t}u(t)\ \ (\operatorname{Re}{s}>-3)\qquad\text{or}\qquad h(t)=-e^{-3t}u(-t)\ \ (\operatorname{Re}{s}<-3).

The differential equation fixes the algebraic form; **causality selects the ROC**. ```fig lap-de-rocs ``` ## Solving a differential equation Use the causal system $H(s)=\frac1{s+3}$, $h(t)=e^{-3t}u(t)$: pole at $-3$, ROC $\operatorname{Re}\{s\}>-3$, causal and stable. Input $x(t)=e^{-2t}u(t)\leftrightarrow\frac1{s+2}$: $$\begin{aligned}&Y(s)\\[6pt]&= H(s)X(s)\\[6pt]&= \frac1{(s+2)(s+3)}\\[6pt]&= \frac1{s+2}-\frac1{s+3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad y(t)=\left(e^{-2t}-e^{-3t}\right)u(t). \end{aligned}$$ ```fig lap-de-response ``` The transform solved the differential equation by turning it into algebra, then using known inverse pairs. ## LCCDEs become algebra For a linear constant-coefficient differential equation

\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_k\frac{d^ky(t)}{dt^k}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_k\frac{d^kx(t)}{dt^k},

zero initial conditions and the differentiation property give $\left(\sum a_ks^k\right)Y(s)=\left(\sum b_ks^k\right)X(s)$, so

\boxed{,H(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_ks^k}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_ks^k},}

Zeros come from the numerator, poles from the denominator, and the ROC still depends on causality or other side information. ## Exam pointers - Every transform needs its ROC; quote it and use it when inverting. - A strip ROC means a two-sided signal: one term with $u(t)$, one with $u(-t)$. - Before declaring a pole, check whether a zero cancels it (finite-duration signals have none). - "Relation between Laplace and Fourier": $X(j\omega)=X(s)|_{s=j\omega}$ if the ROC contains the $j\omega$ axis; in general $X(\sigma+j\omega)=\mathcal F\{x(t)e^{-\sigma t}\}$.
