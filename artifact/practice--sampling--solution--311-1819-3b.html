Its main cause is that practical signals are time-limited and therefore not strictly band-limited, combined with sampling below twice the highest significant frequency.

Problems.

  1. Loss of high-frequency content. The tail of G(f)G(f)G(f) beyond fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 is cut off by the reconstruction filter.
  2. Spectral folding. The tails of neighbouring copies fall inside the baseband. A component at fs/2+Δff_s/2+\Delta ffs​/2+Δf reappears at fs/2−Δff_s/2-\Delta ffs​/2−Δf, as a false low frequency ("frequency in disguise"). Example: sampling a 7 kHz tone at 10 kHz produces a 3 kHz tone after reconstruction. Wagon wheels appearing to spin backwards in films is the same effect.
  3. It cannot be undone. Once components overlap, no filter can separate the true from the aliased content.

Compensation.

  • Anti-aliasing filter. Low-pass filter the signal to B<fs/2B<f_s/2B<fs​/2 before sampling. We lose the components above BBB deliberately, but nothing folds back into the band.
  • Over-sample. Use fs>2Bf_s>2Bfs​>2B, typically 10–25% higher. This creates guard bands so that realisable filters with finite roll-off can be used for both anti-aliasing and reconstruction.
  • Example: telephone speech is band-limited to 3.4 kHz and sampled at 8 kHz rather than 6.8 kHz.
