Frequency response and distortionless systems

Transfer functions, ideal filters, all-pass systems and the distortionless conditions.

8 past questions on this topic

Why this topic

This topic joins LTI systems (topic 3) with the Fourier transform (topic 6). It is not a separate lecture in the slides, but the CSE 311 term finals ask about it almost every year: distortionless transmission, all-pass systems, ideal filters, and why ideal filters cannot be built. Everything here follows from one equation.

Frequency response

For an LTI system with impulse response h(t)h(t)h(t), the frequency response (transfer function) is

H(jω)=∫−∞∞h(t)e−jωtdt,H(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt,H(jω)=∫−∞∞​h(t)e−jωtdt,

and the convolution theorem turns y=x∗hy=x*hy=x∗h into

 Y(jω)=H(jω) X(jω) \boxed{\,Y(j\omega)=H(j\omega)\,X(j\omega)\,}Y(jω)=H(jω)X(jω)​

Gain and phase

Write H(jω)=∣H(jω)∣ ejθ(ω)H(j\omega)=|H(j\omega)|\,e^{j\theta(\omega)}H(jω)=∣H(jω)∣ejθ(ω). A real sinusoid comes out at the same frequency, scaled and shifted:

This also tells us how to measure HHH experimentally:

  • Sinusoidal sweep. Apply sinusoids at many frequencies; record the amplitude ratio (gives ∣H∣|H|∣H∣) and the phase shift (gives θ\thetaθ).
  • Impulse method. Apply a very narrow, tall pulse (an approximate δ\deltaδ), record the output ≈h(t)\approx h(t)≈h(t), and Fourier transform it.
  • Broadband method. Apply a known wide-band input (noise, a chirp, a step) and compute H=Y/XH=Y/XH=Y/X from the measured spectra.

Distortionless transmission

We want the output to keep the shape of the input. Changing the size and arriving later is allowed:

y(t)=k x(t−td).y(t)=k\,x(t-t_d).y(t)=kx(t−td​).

Take the Fourier transform (time-shift property):

Y(jω)=k e−jωtdX(jω)⟹ H(jω)=k e−jωtd,h(t)=k δ(t−td) Y(j\omega)=k\,e^{-j\omega t_d}X(j\omega)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\boxed{\,H(j\omega)=k\,e^{-j\omega t_d},\qquad h(t)=k\,\delta(t-t_d)\,}Y(jω)=ke−jωtd​X(jω)⟹H(jω)=ke−jωtd​,h(t)=kδ(t−td​)​

So a distortionless system has two properties:

  1. Constant magnitude: ∣H(jω)∣=k|H(j\omega)|=k∣H(jω)∣=k. Every frequency gets the same gain.
  2. Linear phase through the origin: θ(ω)=−ωtd\theta(\omega)=-\omega t_dθ(ω)=−ωtd​. Every frequency gets the same delay.

Why linear phase means "equal delay"

A component cos⁡ωt\cos\omega tcosωt shifted in phase by θ(ω)\theta(\omega)θ(ω) is the same as a delay of

td(ω)=−θ(ω)ω.t_d(\omega)=-\frac{\theta(\omega)}{\omega}.td​(ω)=−ωθ(ω)​.

This is the same for every ω\omegaω only if θ\thetaθ is a straight line through the origin. A higher frequency needs proportionally more phase to be delayed by the same amount of time. (Equivalently, the group delay −dθdω-\frac{d\theta}{d\omega}−dωdθ​ must be constant, and θ(0)=0\theta(0)=0θ(0)=0.)

What happens if the conditions fail

  • Amplitude distortion (∣H∣|H|∣H∣ not constant): some bands are boosted or cut. Speech sounds muffled or tinny; pulses widen.
  • Phase (delay) distortion (θ\thetaθ not linear): components arrive at different times. The amplitudes are fine, but the waveform is reshaped.

In data links, either distortion spreads each pulse into its neighbours' time slots: inter-symbol interference. The ear is fairly tolerant of phase errors; video and data receivers are not. Both distortions are linear, so an equaliser Heq=1/HH_{eq}=1/HHeq​=1/H over the band can undo them.

Distortionless only over the band that matters

h(t)=kδ(t−td)h(t)=k\delta(t-t_d)h(t)=kδ(t−td​) for all frequencies would need infinite bandwidth: an ideal impulse response, zero rise time. Every real channel has capacitance, inductance and finite bandwidth, so this is physically unrealisable. In practice we only need

∣H(jω)∣≈kandθ(ω)≈−ωtdfor ∣ω∣<2πB,|H(j\omega)|\approx k\quad\text{and}\quad\theta(\omega)\approx-\omega t_d\qquad\text{for } |\omega|<2\pi B,∣H(jω)∣≈kandθ(ω)≈−ωtd​for ∣ω∣<2πB,

the band actually occupied by the signal.

Every distortionless system is all-pass, but an all-pass system is distortionless only if its phase is also linear. No frequency is attenuated by an all-pass system, yet its non-linear phase can still reshape the waveform.

How to decide whether a system is distortionless: over the signal's band, check that ∣H∣|H|∣H∣ is flat and θ\thetaθ is a straight line through the origin (constant group delay). In time, h(t)h(t)h(t) must be a single scaled, delayed impulse.

Paired echoes: ripple in the gain spreads pulses

H(jω)={(1+kcos⁡ωT) e−jωtd,∣ω∣<2πB0,otherwise.H(j\omega)=\begin{cases}(1+k\cos\omega T)\,e^{-j\omega t_d},&|\omega|<2\pi B\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}H(jω)={(1+kcosωT)e−jωtd​,0,​∣ω∣<2πBotherwise.​

Y=Xe−jωtd+k2Xe−jω(td−T)+k2Xe−jω(td+T),Y=Xe^{-j\omega t_d}+\tfrac k2Xe^{-j\omega(t_d-T)}+\tfrac k2Xe^{-j\omega(t_d+T)},Y=Xe−jωtd​+2k​Xe−jω(td​−T)+2k​Xe−jω(td​+T),

and each linear-phase factor is a time shift:

y(t)=x(t−td)+k2[x(t−td+T)+x(t−td−T)].y(t)=x(t-t_d)+\frac k2\Big[x(t-t_d+T)+x(t-t_d-T)\Big].y(t)=x(t−td​)+2k​[x(t−td​+T)+x(t−td​−T)].

The phase is perfectly linear, yet the ripple in ∣H∣|H|∣H∣ produces two small echoes, one before and one after the main pulse. This is a clean picture of how amplitude distortion spreads energy in time.

Ideal filters

The ideal low-pass filter

H(jω)={e−jωtd,∣ω∣<W0,∣ω∣>W(in hertz: H(f)=Π ⁣(f2B)e−j2πftd, W=2πB)H(j\omega)=\begin{cases}e^{-j\omega t_d},&|\omega|<W\\0,&|\omega|>W\end{cases} \qquad\text{(in hertz: } H(f)=\Pi\!\left(\tfrac f{2B}\right)e^{-j2\pi ft_d},\ W=2\pi B)H(jω)={e−jωtd​,0,​∣ω∣<W∣ω∣>W​(in hertz: H(f)=Π(2Bf​)e−j2πftd​, W=2πB)

Why an ideal filter is physically unrealisable

  • h(t)h(t)h(t) is the response to δ(t)\delta(t)δ(t), applied at t=0t=0t=0. But the sinc is non-zero for t<0t<0t<0: the filter responds before the input arrives. It is non-causal.
  • A larger delay tdt_dtd​ moves the sinc right but never removes its infinite tail to the left.
  • More generally, the Paley–Wiener criterion says a causal filter cannot have ∣H∣=0|H|=0∣H∣=0 over a whole band of frequencies.

Real filters (Butterworth, Chebyshev, elliptic) approximate the brick wall with a finite transition band and a delay.

The edges become finite ramps (rise time about π/W\pi/Wπ/W), there is ringing with about 9% overshoot (the Gibbs phenomenon), and the output begins before the input does. The last point is why the filter is "ideal" rather than "real".
